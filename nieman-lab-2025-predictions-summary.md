# Nieman Lab's 2025 Predictions for Journalism - Comprehensive Summary

Each year, Nieman Lab asks some of the smartest people in journalism and media what they think is coming in the next 12 months. At the end of 2024, they published over 100 expert predictions for 2025. Below is a comprehensive summary of these predictions, organized by theme.

## AI and Technology

### Publishers Find the AI Era Not All That Lucrative
**Author:** Rasmus Kleis Nielsen (Professor of Communication, University of Copenhagen)

Nielsen challenges the optimistic narrative about AI licensing deals, predicting that most publishers will not get any meaningful revenue from licensing content to technology companies. While there have been deals between various publishers and tech companies developing AI, these represent only dozens of deals in a world with many thousands of news publishers. Even for publishers who secured deals like Dotdash Meredith and Axel Springer, continued decline in print revenues alone will have a far bigger impact than their OpenAI deals. Nielsen suggests this realization might be "liberating" for publishers, allowing them to focus on what matters — creating value not for tech companies' training models, but for members of the public who want to make sense of the world beyond personal experience.

### The Year We Stop Talking About 'AI'
**Author:** Burt Herman

Herman argues that rather than worrying about AI taking away jobs or isolating us further in society, the best media products will help us connect and be more human. The prediction suggests that AI will become so integrated into journalism workflows that it will no longer be a separate topic of discussion, but rather a normalized tool that enhances human connection and storytelling.

### AI Turns News Into a Conversation
**Author:** Nikita Roy

Roy predicts that news will transform from something passively consumed to something actively experienced through voice-first AI that turns text into conversation. This represents a fundamental shift in how audiences engage with journalism, moving from reading or watching to interactive dialogue with news content.

### Put AI at the Beginning, Not at the End

This prediction argues that newsrooms should integrate AI at the start of their workflows rather than treating it as an afterthought or final production tool. By placing AI at the beginning of the journalism process, news organizations can leverage its capabilities more effectively for research, data analysis, and story development.

### Newsrooms Will Reckon with AI Slop

As AI-generated content proliferates across the web, newsrooms will face the challenge of distinguishing their quality journalism from low-quality "AI slop." This prediction suggests that 2025 will be the year when newsrooms must actively address how to maintain standards and credibility in an environment increasingly flooded with automated, low-quality content.

### Recalibrating on AI's Promise

After years of hype and experimentation, newsrooms will take a more measured approach to AI, recalibrating expectations and focusing on practical applications that genuinely improve journalism rather than chasing every new AI trend. This reflects a maturation of the industry's relationship with artificial intelligence.

### Divergent Paths for Journalism's Future with AI

This prediction suggests that journalism will split into different approaches to AI adoption, with some organizations embracing it fully, others remaining skeptical, and many finding a middle path. These divergent strategies will create distinct models for how AI integrates into journalism practice.

## Audience and Distribution

### The Mainstream Media Will Lose Its Last Grip on Relevancy
**Author:** Alice Marwick (Director of Research, Data & Society)

Marwick predicts that in 2025, the mainstream media will lose its last grip on relevancy, and the fundamental gap in understanding between partisan Americans will widen. The gap between mainstream media readers, people who get most of their news through influencers or partisan social media, and people who barely think about news at all will create a fundamental schism in how Americans see the world. The 2024 election showed just how irrelevant mainstream news coverage is outside the beltway and chattering class. Rather than seeking it out, people are exposed to snippets of current affairs as part of curated news feeds, often from obscure or disreputable sources, with only 3% of Facebook's content being political news.

### Focusing on What People Actually Need
**Author:** Lynn Walsh (Assistant Director, Trusting News)

Walsh predicts that in 2025, the goal won't just be producing great stories — it'll be making sure those stories reach people in ways that matter to them. That's how journalism will stay relevant and rebuild trust. In 2025, journalism is going to get a much-needed reality check. Instead of sticking to old habits and assuming people will adapt to how journalists deliver news, newsrooms will finally start designing content that fits into real lives — short attention spans and busy schedules. For too long, newsrooms have created content based on their own routines, preferences, and platforms, churning out long stories or endless updates without really thinking about what the audience wants — or can keep up with.

### Embrace the Barbell
**Author:** Millie Tran (Chief Digital Content Officer, Council on Foreign Relations)

Tran argues it's time to abandon middling stories and go very short or very long. The "barbell" concept refers to a content strategy where news organizations focus on producing either very short-form or very long-form content while avoiding medium-length stories. It seems increasingly clear that people are gravitating to extremes in the types of content they consume — whether scrolling short-form posts or listening to hours-long podcasts. When news organizations try to do everything, they think they're showing strength, but they're inadvertently contributing to their own vulnerability, creating a flood of mediocre content that becomes easier to dismiss, easier to replace with less-credible sources, and easier to lose in the noise.

### Why 2025 Is All About User Needs
**Author:** Dmitry Shishkin

Shishkin argues that audience engagement has been narrowly tackled by our industry for far too long, often treated as a side initiative rather than a central purpose of journalism. Newsrooms have been enthusiastically adopting user needs models and leveraging AI tools to conduct relevant analysis. In 2025, the focus will shift to meeting people where they are, offering news that's not just informative but genuinely useful, relatable, and easy to engage with.

### Meet the New Metrics, Same as the Old Metrics
**Author:** Margarita Noriega

Noriega warns that it won't be long before AI is used to generate new metrics and new analysis, and to push user behavior further away from curated editorial experiences. Feed algorithms were introduced to address "what's the difference between when an event occurs and its relevance," transforming how the news business worked, with almost everything online now powered by some version of user experience, data collection, analysis, and recommendations.

### The Rise of Informal News Networks
**Author:** Heather Chaplin

Chaplin discusses how America's formal news structures will continue to decline, with twentieth-century news outlets crashing due to changing business and technological models, hubris, cultural mistrust, and overall irrelevance in people's daily lives. However, news and information won't stop circulating and people won't stop talking to their neighbors about thorny topics. Chaplin provides examples from her research including a barber in Baltimore who hosts casual discussion groups while cutting hair, a UPS driver in Central California who delivers sensitive information along with packages, and a woman in Detroit who documents neighborhood evictions in a self-published newsletter. She predicts that informal news networks like these will play increasingly important roles in the near future.

### Declining Social Media Traffic Will Continue

One prediction states that the declining level of traffic from social media will likely continue to decline in 2025, attributed not just to changes in the platforms themselves, but to how the public has changed. The public simply doesn't use social media the way they used to, and news in particular is a really bad fit for that changed behavior. The focus many publishers have started to put on TikTok is unlikely to lead to any real monetary improvement, and channels like Threads or Bluesky will grow but not at the scale we used to think about social media, making them more or less irrelevant when it comes to the actual profitability of publishers.

## Content Creators and Influencers

### Influencers Become Journalists
**Author:** Jessica Maddox

Maddox notes that a November 2024 Pew Research Center study found 21% of U.S. adults rely on news influencers for information. She argues that journalists are influencers, and influencers are journalists, though suggests thinking of them as op-ed writers or cultural commentators if that's easier to accept. For many younger audiences, the "news anchor" is now a TikTok personality, a YouTube commentator, or a podcaster with a loyal following. Among adults under 30, that jumps to 38%.

### Content Creators Find a Place in Newsrooms
**Author:** Julia Munslow

Munslow envisions a new type of journalist in newsrooms across the country: a content creator who acts as a modern-day news anchor who has perfected the skill of connecting with online audiences and serves as a bridge between the newsroom and the public. She notes that when you ask someone in your life under 40 where they get the news, odds are they'll mention a non-newsroom podcaster or YouTuber.

### Your Audience Team Is Now Your Creator Team
**Author:** Ryan Kellett

Kellett predicts that the standalone Audience team/department/function, as we know it, is dead. Audience teams should no longer be siloed but integrated across organizations, with chief audience officers appointed to break down silos. He suggests that the best "audiencers" are busy running head first into what's next: creator journalists or what Pew calls news influencers.

### Embracing Influencers as Allies
**Author:** Marlon A. Walker

Walker predicts that news organizations will increasingly rely on digital creators not just as amplifiers but as integral partners in storytelling. This represents a shift from viewing influencers as competition or a threat to traditional journalism, toward seeing them as collaborators who can help reach audiences that traditional media struggles to engage.

## Print Revival

### A Renewed Appetite for Print
**Author:** Aimee Rinehart (Senior Product Manager of AI Strategy, Associated Press)

Rinehart predicts that in 2025, people will have a renewed appetite for print publications. Ladina Heimgartner, CEO of Ringier Media Switzerland, said at the WAN-IFRA Congress in Copenhagen that print is the new luxury, a symbol of leisure and remedy to the daily digital grind. Print offers serendipity and discovery compared to algorithm-driven digital feeds.

### The Print Revival Comes to News
**Author:** Esther Kezia Thorpe (Co-Founder, The Publisher Podcast & Newsletter by Media Voices)

Thorpe notes that while daily newspapers will continue their decline, there's a real opportunity for news in a printed form in 2025. There has been a swathe of print magazine launches, relaunches, and frequency increases this year, leading to the general acceptance that there is something of a print revival, generally confined to magazines as readers turn to trusted expertise and seek physical reading experiences.

## Business Models and Revenue

### Back to the Bundle
**Author:** Ben Smith

Smith expects an annual Substack subscription to hit in 2025 as individual publishers struggle to survive the coming wave of churning unsubscriptions. The consumer push for bundling is obvious: the number of subscriptions you need to stay informed across entertainment and news is insane, unmanageable, and expensive. The prediction suggests that just as the media landscape fragmented into individual Substacks and podcasts, there are only two ways to go in media: bundling and unbundling, and the pendulum is swinging back toward bundling.

### Impact Investment Enters the Chat
**Author:** Adam Thomas (Founder, Evenly Distributed)

Thomas predicts that impact investment will enter the journalism sector in 2025. Impact investment has largely avoided journalism because media is often seen as high-risk and tough-to-scale with uncertain returns, with limited data on successful exits and poorly understood societal value. Organizations like V-Ventures, Karma Capital, and Media Development Investment Fund have already begun to turn systemic barriers to media investment into new opportunities. Beyond 2025, Thomas envisions a flourishing landscape of adequately financed, equitable media enterprises that deliver impactful content, where media investment will be seen as a vital pillar for democracy rather than high-risk, proving that mission-driven journalism is something to invest in.

### The Publisher Is Always Right
**Author:** Gabe Schneider

Schneider warns that the business model that sustained newsrooms for the last century is over, and we're left with the rich buying up newspapers like hometown sports teams. Unless we come together as a journalism field and course-correct away from information consolidation controlled by the ultra-wealthy, it will get worse. He suggests alternatives like stronger union-run newsrooms, news cooperatives, nonprofits, or even significantly more government investment in news.

### Journalists Will Learn to Stop Worrying and Love the Business Plan
**Author:** Shira T. Center

Center predicts that journalists will continue to take matters into their own hands — or spreadsheets — either as entrepreneurs striking out on their own or innovators in established and legacy institutions. The shift from advertising to a subscription or membership-based business model has made business planning more palpable to journalists.

### You'll Get What You Pay For
**Author:** Sara Morrison

Morrison discusses how major outlets like Vox, The Verge, CNN, and Reuters have recently announced paywall plans. The prediction argues that free content will be increasingly synonymous with AI slop and other low-quality content, and that a paywall tells readers that there's something behind it that's worth paying for.

## Political Coverage and Democracy

### The New York Times Will Hire a Pro-Trump Columnist
**Author:** Matthew Pressman (Associate Professor of Journalism, Seton Hall University)

Pressman notes that New York Times publisher A. G. Sulzberger stated in December 2023 that the paper has "a far more diverse mix of opinions, including more conservative and heterodox voices, than ever before," yet there are no voices representing MAGA, the dominant strain of thought on the right. Pressman expects this to change in 2025, noting that Sulzberger and executive editor Joe Kahn have expressed a dogged insistence on hewing to the ideas of objectivity and balance. He draws a historical parallel to 1973 when the Times added William Safire, a speechwriter for Nixon and Spiro Agnew, who would go directly from bashing news outlets on behalf of a dishonest, press-hating administration to one of the most prestigious posts in American journalism.

### An Authoritarian Anti-Journalism Playbook
**Author:** Benjamin Toff

Toff wrote that Trump has shown a willingness to borrow from the playbook of authoritarians elsewhere, and what happens over the next year to American journalism will almost certainly be imported from abroad. He argues that the best, most rigorous journalism in the world is powerless to effect change unless it reaches and is trusted by the wider public, highlighting concerns about journalism's effectiveness in the face of authoritarian threats.

### Newsrooms Reinvent Their Political Journalism
**Author:** Alexandra Borchardt

Borchardt argues that chances are in 2025, newsrooms will finally rethink the paradigm of political journalism. Reporting on racist, misogynist, dehumanizing opinions and comments has the opposite effect from what most journalists intend — normalizing propaganda and even making political candidates seem interesting. Her recommendations include studying human behavior and chasing data, not just quotes, and connecting reporting to people's everyday lives, as politicians have an agenda and journalists are often swayed by it, while people are likely to have different ones.

### Newsrooms Fight Back Against Criminalization
**Author:** Jose Zamora

Zamora predicted that attacks on journalists are set to escalate in 2025, noting that the criminalization of journalism is a disturbing global trend that has been perfected by authoritarian regimes in places like Nicaragua, Venezuela, Russia, China, and the Philippines.

### Media's Acquiescence to Trump Will Fail
**Author:** Noah Shachtman

Shachtman discussed a movement in media c-suites to get right with Donald Trump and predicted it's going to fail badly. The prediction addresses concerns about media organizations choosing compliance over their watchdog role.

### The Media Surrenders to Trump 2.0
**Author:** Parker Molloy

Molloy argues that media organizations are choosing compliance with eyes wide open, trading their watchdog role for a seat at the billionaire's table. The prediction notes that the coming wave of media consolidation means these owners have a vested interest in keeping Trump happy.

### Progressive Media at a Crossroads
**Author:** Anthony Nadler

Nadler predicts that 2025 will see major growth in online progressive media, though what's harder to predict is which communities will see themselves represented in this media. The 2024 election highlighted the power of the right-wing media ecosystem and the parasocial bonds people build with media personalities, leading to a push in 2025 to start building a competing media sphere among left/progressive/center-left activists, entrepreneurs, and donors.

### The Political Journalism That Matters Most Will Be Built From the Ground Up

This prediction hopes that the outlets that matter most to democracy move away from reflexive "gotcha" coverage of politicians and toward a practice that centers voters' lived experience, allows idealism back into the frame, and treats accountability as something deeper than a takedown.

### Right-Wing Studies' Time Has Come
**Author:** A.J. Bauer

Bauer discusses how by focusing on misinformation and disinformation, journalists and scholars found a seemingly apolitical and nonpartisan solution to a fundamentally political problem. He argues that factual accuracy is a necessary but insufficient goal, and that journalists need to compile facts into a vision of reality that is more compelling than that offered by right-wing media.

## Misinformation and Trust

### Getting Beyond the Fact-Check
**Author:** Cristina Tardáguila (Founder, Lupa, Brazil)

Tardáguila predicts that journalism's fight against disinformation risks irrelevance if it fails to consider how the human mind processes and reacts to both falsehoods and facts. By understanding these cognitive dynamics, journalism can design strategies that truly resonate and drive change.

### All Eyes Are on Brazil
**Author:** Natalia Viana

Viana predicts that if humanity is to overcome the unprecedented threat of weaponized disinformation and digital populism, Brazil is an essential fort to be held, highlighting Brazil's critical role in the global fight against misinformation.

### Following Utter Failure
**Author:** Damon Krukowski

Krukowski notes that far too many in the country were uninformed, misinformed, or simply lied to about topics that journalism is meant to communicate clearly, truthfully, and widely, reflecting on journalism's failure to effectively combat misinformation.

## Local News

### Prepare for the Unthinkable
**Author:** Jennifer Brandel

Brandel predicts that local journalists, editors, and news leaders will find themselves navigating various crises in their communities, including climate disasters, economic collapse, cultural threats, infectious diseases, and cybersecurity issues. If newsrooms prioritize strengthening relationships with other trusted organizations and individuals who provide civic information, both their communities and their organizations will be more resilient.

### AI Will Reinvent Local News

This prediction suggests that artificial intelligence will transform local news operations, potentially helping under-resourced local newsrooms scale their operations and serve their communities more effectively.

### Nonprofits Step Up to Hold Officials Accountable
**Author:** Kevin D. Grant

Grant predicts that nonprofit newsrooms will increasingly take on the watchdog role of holding government officials and institutions accountable as traditional for-profit local newspapers continue to decline.

## Podcasts and Audio

### Podcasting Becomes the Primary Strategy, Not an Afterthought

This prediction argues that media outlets need to refocus their energies around growing dedicated podcasts as audiences increasingly show their preference for in-depth storytelling and personality-driven conversations. Podcasts can help long-standing organizations establish stronger relationships with audiences at a time when traditional media trust continues to hit historic lows.

### The Podcast Middle Class Will Continue to Shrink
**Author:** Alex Sujong Laughlin

Laughlin predicted that the podcast middle class will continue to shrink, with "The Rogans and Coopers of the world floating in their opulent pools while the rest of us fight for greedy sips of what's left," highlighting growing inequality in the podcast ecosystem.

### Video-First "Podcasts" Bring a New Kind of Coverage
**Author:** John Saroff

Saroff predicts 2025 will bring a video-first news podcast that will combine compelling imagery, quality news gathering, and timely reporting to bring the clarity and scope we currently expect from television news directly to our personal devices. According to a recent study by Pew, 50% of Americans consumed news through podcasts in 2024, up 9% since 2021.

### Podcast Collaboration — Not Competition — Breeds Excellence
**Author:** Joni Deutsch

Deutsch predicted that podcast collaboration rather than competition will breed excellence, suggesting a more cooperative approach to podcast production and distribution.

## Platform Independence

### Declaring Platform Independence
**Author:** Jason Koebler

Koebler's prediction focuses on how the fracturing of the internet has been painful, but it's also given journalism the chance to rebuild by focusing on shared humanity. The last few years have been plagued by the collapse of discoverability on the internet, AI slop and bots making the internet noisy and hard to navigate, social media fracturing, and difficulty sustaining an ad-supported media business by doing commodity journalism.

### Stop Funding Platform Dependence — Start Funding Community Independence
**Author:** Tracie Powell

Powell emphasizes moving away from platform dependence toward community independence, arguing that journalism should invest in owned channels and direct community relationships rather than relying on social media platforms.

## Community Engagement and Trust

### We'll Rethink Scale, Trust, and Our Life's Work
**Author:** S. Mitra Kalita

Kalita focuses on how to gain trust with communities by helping people directly. When you help people once, they'll call you again, and that action represents trust. In mainstream media, newsrooms twisted themselves into knots asking why audiences didn't trust them.

### Redesign News Infrastructures to Build Community Power
**Author:** Jennifer Choi

Choi emphasizes that working towards an explicit North Star goal to build community power is critical as journalism enters possibly the most hostile landscape for journalism we've seen in a while.

### Engagement 2.0
**Author:** Anita Li

Li describes "engagement 2.0" as leveling up community engagement best practices to encourage the public to move from engaging with the news to engaging with the world — that is, their neighborhood, city, province/state, country — around them. Journalism should be information that's useful or user-friendly, that helps people navigate their lives on a daily basis, that helps them make decisions impacting their future, that helps them take action.

## Public Media

### Public Media Reconciles Its Past with the Present
**Author:** Kristen Muller (Former Chief Content Officer, LAist)

Muller notes that when public media makes the news these days, it's usually in the form of a negative headline. Accusations of liberal bias have made organizations like PBS and NPR the target of right-wing efforts to defund the entire system. Reports of audience and revenue decline have left newsroom leaders "fried and frozen." Her prediction for 2025 is that civic leaders, educators, artists, and scientists of all political stripes can convene the modern-day equivalent of the Carnegie Commission to reimagine and redesign a new, networked media space, as preserving public broadcasting in its current form is not an option.

### Public Media's Next Act

This prediction discusses the challenges facing public media stations and their potential future roles as they navigate identity crises and funding challenges.

## Sports Journalism

### Sports Journalists Break Away From Access-Based Journalism
**Author:** Brian Moritz

Moritz predicts that 2025 will be the year when sports journalists start to really reckon with the fact that teams and players don't need them anymore. He cites an October incident where ESPN wanted to do a story about Philadelphia Phillies outfielder Nick Castellanos and his connection to an infamous sports meme, but Castellanos refused because "they wouldn't give me editorial rights of the story to make sure that I liked it before it came out." The idea that an athlete refused to do a story with ESPN because they wanted editorial control is a portent of things to come, as athletes and teams increasingly have their own direct-to-fan communication channels.

## Data Journalism and Investigations

### Visual Investigations Become a Mainstay of News
**Author:** Ståle Grut

Grut predicts that visual investigations are poised to move from niche desks to standard operating procedure in newsrooms in 2025, representing the mainstreaming of investigative techniques that combine traditional reporting with visual analysis and open-source intelligence.

## Diversity and Inclusion

### DEI Dies in Darkness, Taking Integrity With It
**Author:** Danielle C. Belton

Belton's 2026 prediction discusses how many corporate newsrooms have moved away from diversity, equity, and inclusion, shuttering diverse news verticals like NBC BLK or eliminating roles once held by Black people and other historically marginalized groups. If corporate, conglomerate-owned news organizations are willing to abandon their principles around diversity, equity, and inclusion, they'll happily abandon their principles around almost anything.

### The Quest for Diversity Evolves

This prediction reflects on the future of diversity, belonging, equity, and inclusion (DBEI) in journalism, noting that DBEI work has survived being en vogue, falling out of favor, then welcomed back again several times.

### A Year for Revolutionary Journalism
**Author:** Errin Haines

Haines states that the attacks on journalism we are witnessing, from political intimidation to disinformation and media consolidation, are deeply tied to attacks on diversity, equity, and inclusion across American life.

## Youth Audiences

### Long-Form Video Is the Next Big Thing for Young Audiences

This prediction challenges the assumption that young people only want short-form content, noting that Gen Z regularly consumes long-form content through podcasts, YouTube, or Twitch, with YouTube being Gen Z's default video home. About half of trending YouTube videos now resemble traditional TV with long interviews and game shows, suggesting that an appetite for long-form content among young people is on the rise.

### Young Journalists Force the Industry to Change — Whether It's Ready or Not

This prediction states that newsrooms will realize the future of journalism will be shaped from the bottom up by young reporters who are closest to the communities journalism has struggled to reach. These young journalists' work is described as visual, conversational, identity aware, and collaborative, with an audience-first mindset.

## Additional Predictions

### We Can't Predict the Future — But It's Essential to Plan for It
**Author:** AX Mina

Mina states that in our increasingly complex world, to really think about the future of news, we have to think across disciplines: technology platforms, youth trends, global public health, and civic institutions.

### Should We Predict or Define?

This meta-prediction questions whether journalism should simply predict trends or actively work to define and shape the future of the industry.

### Prediction Markets Go Mainstream

This prediction discusses how prediction markets like Kalshi and Polymarket will become more integrated into news coverage and analysis, offering new ways to gauge public sentiment and forecast events.

### We'll Draw the Lines We Will Not Cross

This prediction focuses on journalism's need to establish clear ethical boundaries and principles in an increasingly challenging media environment.

### Journalism Education Leads the Change We Seek
**Author:** Mira Lowe (Dean, School of Journalism & Graphic Communication, Florida A&M University)

Lowe emphasizes that training the next generation of journalists means preparing them to be global citizens. By reinforcing ethics and combating misinformation, fostering global perspectives, integrating AI, saving local news, and ensuring diversity and belonging, journalism education in 2025 will prepare well-rounded, ethically minded, and global students to meet the challenges of a transformative era.

### The Death of the Media Darling
**Author:** P. Kim Bui

Bui's prediction suggests that 2025 will be a year with fewer shooting stars, but we'll start building constellations in a broader night sky, indicating a shift from individual media successes to more collaborative, networked approaches.

### Journalists Fight Digital Decay

This prediction addresses how journalists will work to preserve and maintain digital content as link rot, platform changes, and disappearing archives threaten the historical record.

### Information Becomes Malleable

This prediction warns that the line between fact and fiction will become increasingly blurred as AI tools make it easier to manipulate information, creating new challenges for journalism.

---

## Summary

Nieman Lab's 2025 Predictions for Journalism reveal an industry at a critical crossroads. The dominant themes include AI's disruptive impact on news production and distribution, the decline of mainstream media's relevance among large segments of the population, the rise of influencers and creator journalists, platform independence movements, challenges to traditional business models, authoritarian threats to press freedom, and ongoing struggles with trust and misinformation.

The predictions reflect both pessimism about the collapse of traditional journalism structures and optimism about new approaches focused on community engagement, user needs, and direct audience relationships. Several contributors emphasize the need for journalism to fundamentally rethink its approach to political coverage, business models, and audience engagement to remain relevant and sustainable.

The complete collection is available at [Nieman Lab's Predictions for Journalism 2025](https://www.niemanlab.org/collection/predictions-2025/).
